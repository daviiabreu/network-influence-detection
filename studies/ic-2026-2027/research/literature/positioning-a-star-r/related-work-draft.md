## Related work

**Confronting opinion dynamics models with survey data.** Work calibrating these models
against survey data often finds a weak signal. Gestefeld and Lorenz [@gestefeld2023calibrating] fit an
opinion dynamics model to European Social Survey distributions and to more
than 50,000 annual opinion transitions in the Swiss Household Panel. They note
that validating the mechanism lies beyond what such data can deliver, because
the data record no communication between individuals. Their goodness of fit is
measured against a binomial null rather than a persistence null.
Moor-Smith and Carpentras [@moorsmith2026testing] calibrate four models on
earlier waves of the European Social Survey and validate them on later ones,
normalising performance against a "no change" null. They report that a
minimum amount of drift is needed before a model can beat that null. Under
added noise, and in the empirical series, the optimiser learns to "freeze",
reproducing the null. Their analysis uses aggregate cross-sectional distributions. Working
with individual panel data from a student cohort, Nurek et al.
[@nurek2024coding] compare an influence model against trivial baselines
(keeping one's opinion, random change, majority). They find that the "keep"
baseline maximises overall accuracy while detecting no change, and that
opinion dynamics models often fail to outperform such baselines on real data.
We take this result as our starting point and ask how strong influence must
be before it can be told apart from persistence in a panel of fixed geometry.

**Measurement, response error and the stability of attitudes.** Two
literatures show that the observation channel shapes the conclusions drawn
from such comparisons. In agent-based modelling, Carpentras and Quayle show that random noise
and monotone distortions of the response scale change model predictions
substantially [@carpentras2022propagation], so that poor predictions are not
necessarily evidence of a poor model [@carpentras2023houseofmirrors]. In sociology and
political science, the question of whether observed change is real or is
response instability goes back to Converse's black-and-white model, which
Hill and Kriesi [@hill2001extension] extended with a group of genuine opinion
changers. Kiley and Vaisey [@kiley2020measuring] formalise active updating
and settled dispositions for three-wave panels. They note that frequent
updating becomes indistinguishable from settled dispositions with high
measurement error. Lersch [@lersch2023change] shows by simulation that
persistent change cannot be reliably detected in a three-wave panel, and
turns to long household panels, the Swiss Household Panel among them.
Gelastopoulos, Sage and van de Rijt [@gelastopoulos2025reinforcement] prove
a stronger result: any longitudinal data with systematic between-person
differences can also be generated by a pure reinforcement process. We build
on these results rather than rediscover them. Response error is a separate
channel with externally fixed reliability, and the persistence nulls include
trait-plus-autoregressive and mover–stayer structures. Failure to separate is
therefore read as compatibility with persistence, never as observational
equivalence in general.

**Power and sensitivity in longitudinal designs.** It is well established
that the ability to detect within-person or social effects depends on design
and measurement. For the random-intercept cross-lagged panel model, Mulder
[@mulder2023powriclpm] provides simulation-based power analysis over sample
size, number of waves, between-person variance and indicator reliability.
Dang and Ma [@dang2026fitting] show that power to detect within-person
cross-lagged effects depends jointly on reliability, intraclass correlation,
waves and sample size. Lucas [@lucas2023crosslagged] shows that omitting
stable traits produces spurious cross-lagged effects, which motivates our
trait-plus-autoregressive null. For social influence proper, Stadtfeld et al.
[@stadtfeld2020power] estimate power for selection and influence in
stochastic actor-oriented models with observed networks, and find low power
for influence in typical designs. These studies test the significance of a coefficient within one model. Our
target differs: the minimal
strength at which influence-generated panels become separable from a family
of no-influence generators, under a fixed discriminator, as a function of
reliability.

**Identifiability and detectability of mechanisms.** A parallel line asks
when mechanisms or parameters can be recovered from dynamics. Lenti, Monti
and De Francisci Morales [@lenti2024likelihood] estimate bounded-confidence
parameters by likelihood under full observation, partial observation of
interactions and noisy opinion proxies. Borile et al. [@borile2026bias]
characterise bias and identifiability of these estimators. Tang, Snijders and
Flache [@tang2025empirical] separate bounded confidence from negative
influence using longitudinal network data. Ureña-Carrion, Peixoto and Iñiguez
[@urenacarrion2026statistical] map the detectability of binary spreading
mechanisms across parameter space when the network is known and node states
are fully observed. They show that model selection can fail under common
conditions. These studies assume observed interactions or networks, or work
within a single model class. Our setting is the one faced by analysts of
household panels: exposure is unobserved, opinions are recorded through a
noisy ordinal channel, and the alternative is persistence rather than another
influence rule. Without observed exposure, social influence is not causally
identified, so we frame the result as separability of dynamic patterns.

**Position of this study.** Our documented search covered OpenAlex, Semantic
Scholar and arXiv on 27 September 2026 (queries and screening in the
supplementary material). It did not identify a study that estimates a
detection boundary for influence strength as a function of response
reliability against matched persistence nulls. Absence from a bounded search
is not proof of absence. The contribution we claim is the conjunction of an
interaction-driven generator without observed exposure, a response channel
with fixed reliability, a family of persistence nulls matched on
observed lag-1 and lag-2 autocorrelations, and a pre-registered discriminator
whose threshold is reported as a finite operational curve a*(r). Because the
discriminator's AUC bounds the Bayes-optimal AUC from below, a*(r) is an
upper bound on the optimal threshold, and non-attainment does not establish
indistinguishability.
